What Global Diagnosis is

Global Diagnosis is a treatment-planning system from J. William Robbins and Jeffrey S. Rouse. It builds on facially generated treatment planning, which starts from where the incisal edges belong in the face. The authors found that edges can be in the right place and the result still fail, because the gingival levels or the smile frame are wrong.

So the system starts from one question: why are the gingival levels where they are? Once the cause is named, only a few treatments fit it. In the authors' words, the diagnosis leads to the treatment plan. Pulpal, periodontal and occlusal findings still matter, but as "regional" diagnoses that adjust the plan rather than set it.

  1. MEASUREThe Global Analysis Diagnosis (GAD) form, outside in, in about 5 minutes
  2. ASK 5The five CORE questions explain the discrepancy
  3. NAME 4Skeletal, lip, dentoalveolar extrusion, or short/long tooth
  4. CHOOSE FROM 6Graft, crown lengthening, intrusion, extrusion, surgery, plastics

How to use the tool

The Exam tab follows the GAD form from the face inward. Fill it in at the chair in this order. Any field can stay blank; the read-out on the right only uses what you enter.

  1. Set up. Seat the patient upright in natural head position, ideally standing for the face and midline views. Have a digital caliper or millimeter ruler, a periodontal probe and an explorer.

  2. Patient. Enter sex and age. The lip and display norms depend on both, and lip norms shift 1 mm per decade after 40.

  3. Q1 Face. With lips and teeth slightly apart, measure the middle third (glabella to subnasale) and lower third (subnasale to menton). Add the Angle class, and from the profile, how far each lip sits behind the nose–chin E-line.

  4. Q2 Lip. In repose, measure lip length at the midline and how much central incisor shows. If the lip covers the edge, record a negative number. Then ask the patient to laugh; "smile" usually gets a clenched grimace. Record gingival display above the margins, and measure mobility directly if you can.

  5. Q3 Gingival line. Look at the line through the canine and central gingival margins against the horizon: parallel, concave or canted. To judge the incisal plane, stretch the upper lip level with your fingers and use it as the horizon.

  6. Q4–5 Teeth. Measure crown length on #5–#12. On teeth that measure short, also record the width and feel for the CEJ with an explorer. A CEJ you can't feel on a short tooth means altered passive eruption.

  7. Smile frame and context. Midline, wear, buccal corridors and the rest of the form enrich the note. Mark whether orthodontics or anterior restorations are planned; that unlocks the sequencing advice.

  8. Copy the note. Press Copy note and paste it into the chart or case presentation. Press Clear form before the next patient. Nothing is saved, so refreshing the page erases everything.

Reading the result

Five CORE questions

Each question shows Normal Outside norm or Not recorded. Diagnoses appear once three or more are answered.

Global diagnoses

One card per cause. The small label gives the family, the line under the title shows the measurement that triggered it, and the chips list the primary and secondary treatments from the book.

Tool numbers

1 graft · 2 crown lengthening · 3 intrusion · 4 extrusion · 5 surgery · 6 plastics. R restorative, O orthodontics, E education, exercises or appliance.

Sequencing and other findings

Sequencing appears when orthodontics or restorations are planned, or when there is more than one diagnosis. Other findings are not global diagnoses but still belong in the plan: midline, W:L ratio, wear, an aged smile.

Several diagnoses at once is normal, and it's the point: a gummy smile often has more than one cause, and each cause needs its own treatment. No global diagnosis means the gingival levels are explained by normal anatomy. Incomplete means too few questions were answered to say.

Worked example: a 27-year-old woman who dislikes her gummy smile

  • Q1: lower third 70 mm against a middle third of 61 mm → vertical maxillary excess.
  • Q2: lip 19 mm, below the 20–22 mm norm → short upper lip. Calculated mobility of 8.5 mm → hypermobile lip, flagged because it wasn't measured directly.
  • Q4–5: centrals 9 mm with no palpable CEJ → altered passive eruption.

How to read it: the 5 mm of gingival display has four contributors. Crown lengthening corrects only the altered passive eruption. The skeletal excess needs a Le Fort I impaction, or Botox if she declines surgery. Measure lip mobility directly before deciding on Botox for the lip.

Limits

  • A teaching aid. Clinical judgment, photographs, mounted casts and radiographs still govern the plan.
  • The book gives the questions, diagnoses and normal values but no single cut-off algorithm. The rules here put the book into practice and are listed on the Reference tab. The ±5% tolerance on the 1:1 face ratio belongs to this tool, not the book.
  • Calculated lip mobility runs high when crowns are short or little tooth shows at rest. Measure it directly when it decides treatment.
  • Display norms come from young adults, and incisal display falls with age.

4 global diagnoses

  1. Skeletal discrepancy: vertical maxillary excess (VME) or deficiency (VMD), Class II or III.
  2. Upper lip: short, long, hypermobile or hypomobile.
  3. Dentoalveolar extrusion: the gingival line is concave or canted instead of parallel to the horizon. Subtyped as without wear, with wear, or from aberrant growth or attrition.
  4. Short or long tooth: short from altered passive eruption, attrition or microdontia; long from recession.

5 CORE questions

  1. What are the facial proportions and skeletal relationships?
  2. What are the length and mobility of the upper lip?
  3. What is the relationship between the gingival line and the horizon?
  4. What is the length of the maxillary central incisor?
  5. Is the CEJ palpable in the gingival sulcus?

6 tools

  1. Tissue grafting for root coverage
  2. Crown lengthening
  3. Orthodontic intrusion
  4. Orthodontic extrusion
  5. Surgery: orthognathic, extraction, implant
  6. Plastics: Botox, fillers, plastic surgery

Normative values

How this tool decides

  • VME: lower third longer than the middle third (ch 3 defines it this way). If the smile isn't gummy, the book treats it as no esthetic problem.
  • VMD: lower third shorter than the middle third.
  • Class II / III: Angle class, or an E-line screen when the class isn't recorded: lower lip more than 2 mm behind suggests Class II; in front suggests Class III.
  • Short / long lip: outside F 20–22 or M 22–24 mm, shifted 1 mm per decade after 40. Hyper- / hypomobile: mobility above 8 or below 6 mm.
  • DAE: concave gingival line (with or without wear) or a canted line (aberrant growth or attrition).
  • APE: a short tooth whose CEJ can't be felt in the sulcus.
  • Short central (<10 mm): incisal attrition when pathologic wear is recorded; microdontia when the CEJ is palpable and there is no wear.
  • Long tooth: recession on any tooth #5–#12.
  • When mobility isn't measured, the book's arithmetic is used: (central length − repose display) + smile gingival display.

Diagnoses and treatments follow chapter 3 of the book; sequencing follows chapter 11. The book gives no tolerance for the 1:1 face ratio, so the ±5% is this tool's.
